Mereb has one policy that clients notice before anything else: **we do not staff juniors on
client work.** Every engineer we place has six-plus years of production experience behind them.

It reads like a sales line. It started as one. Then we had to actually run it, and running it
forced us to answer a question most engineering organisations avoid: what is the bar, in words,
such that two different interviewers reach the same verdict?

Here is where we landed.

## Years are a proxy, and a weak one

Six years is what we say publicly because it filters the inbox. It is not the thing we test
for. I have met engineers with three years who clear our bar and engineers with twelve who
don’t, and the difference is never syntax.

The difference is what happens when the problem is underspecified.

Hand a mid-level engineer an ambiguous ticket and you get a question back. That is correct
behaviour — asking is better than guessing. Hand the same ticket to a senior engineer and you
get a decision back, with the two options they rejected, the tradeoff they accepted, and the
way to reverse it if they were wrong.

That is the whole test. Everything else on our scorecard is downstream of it.

## The four things we actually check

**Can they make a call and own it?** We give candidates a real ambiguity from a real project —
the sort where both answers are defensible. We are not grading the answer. We are grading
whether they noticed it was a decision, and whether they wrote down why.

**Can they read code faster than they write it?** In an embedded team, you spend more time in
someone else’s codebase than in your own. Reviewing a pull request well is a harder skill than
authoring one, and it is the one that compounds across a team.

**Do they degrade gracefully?** Not the code — the person. Production is on fire, the client is
awake, the cause is not where you thought. Some engineers get sharper and narrower. Some get
broader and faster and start changing things. Only one of those is who you want holding the
pager.

**Can they write?** Not prose style. Can they leave behind an explanation that a colleague six
time zones away can act on without a call. In a distributed org this is not a soft skill; it is
the primary interface.

## Why the policy is real and not a slogan

The commercial pressure to break it is constant, and it comes from both directions.

From our side: a junior is cheaper, and there is always a client who would take the discount.
From the client’s side: someone always asks whether we can "just put a couple of juniors on the
simpler tickets."

We say no, for a reason that has nothing to do with the juniors. **An embedded team’s value is
its context.** After six months inside a client’s product, the team knows why a table has that
weird column and which service you must never deploy on a Friday. That knowledge is the
product. Anything that dilutes it — rotation, understaffing seniority, treating people as
interchangeable seats — destroys the thing the client is paying for.

Juniors are not a problem. Juniors on someone else’s critical path, without the mentorship
structure to catch them, is a problem. We train people; we do so on our own time and our own
risk, which is where that cost belongs.

## What this has to do with Addis Ababa

The stated reason companies hesitate about African engineering teams is quality variance. It is
not a stupid concern. The outsourcing industry earned it, globally, over thirty years of
selling seats rather than engineers.

The way to answer it is not a pitch deck. It is a bar you can describe in writing, apply
consistently, and be held to — and then a portfolio of clients whose CTOs will take the
reference call.

There is no shortage of engineers in this city who clear that bar. There is a shortage of
companies who have looked, and a shortage of firms willing to be specific about what looking
means.

So: no juniors on client work. In writing, and it costs us deals.
