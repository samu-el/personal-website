Before Mereb was a company, it was a handful of us in Addis Ababa taking contracts
individually. Good work, paid on time, no shortage of it. In 2021 we stopped doing that and
started an outsourcing company instead.

The code did not get harder. Everything around the code did, and in ways I did not anticipate.

## What freelancing cannot do

Freelancing is efficient and it has a hard ceiling, which you hit in three specific places.

**You cannot take the work you are best at.** The interesting engagements — the ones where a
client wants a team inside their product for two years — require an entity that will still
exist in two years and can put four people on it. An individual cannot bid on that, no matter
how good they are.

**You cannot say no in a way that means anything.** A freelancer who turns down a bad-fit
project has turned down income. A company that turns it down is expressing a standard. Those
look identical from outside and are completely different internally.

**You cannot build a bar.** This is the one that decided it. Individually, quality is a fact
about you. Collectively, it has to become a written, teachable, enforceable thing — or it
regresses to the mean of whoever you hired last.

## What got hard immediately

**Hiring became the job.** I write less code than I did in 2020 and I think about hiring more
than about architecture. That is the correct trade and it took me a year to stop resenting it.
Every engineer you place is a standing claim about your judgement.

**Continuity became an engineering concern.** A freelancer finishing a project moves on, and
that is the deal. A company running a two-year embedded engagement cannot rotate people
without deleting the context that makes the team valuable. Retention stopped being an HR topic
and became something I design around.

**The reference call became the product.** Nobody in Germany buys an Ethiopian engineering team
from a website. They buy it because another CTO told them it worked. That means every
engagement is a sales asset or a liability, with no third category — a much sharper incentive
than any contract clause.

**"Outsourcing" arrived with thirty years of baggage.** The word means body-shop to a lot of
buyers, and they are not wrong about the industry’s history. We spend real effort explaining
that we sell teams and not seats, and the only argument that lands is a client willing to say
it for us.

## What I would tell someone about to do this

Decide early whether you are selling capacity or capability, and then let it cost you money.

Selling capacity is a volume business: fill seats, keep utilisation high, compete on rate. It
works. It is not what we wanted, and every commercial pressure pushes you toward it — the
client who wants a discount for juniors, the quarter where a warm body would close the gap.

We staff no juniors on client work. That policy has cost us deals, which is how I know it is a
real policy and not a page on a website.

The other thing: get very good at writing. A distributed engineering organisation running
across six time zones is, functionally, a writing organisation. The decisions that survive are
the written ones. The engineers who compound fastest are the ones who explain themselves well.
I did not expect that to be the central skill, and it is.
